Our Community

A century of self-reliance in northwest Houston.

Acres Homes is one of the oldest and largest historically Black communities in Texas — founded on the idea that families deserved room to build, grow, and own.

History

Founded on land, and on independence

Acres Homes was established during World War I, when landowners began selling homesites large enough for a garden, chickens, or farm animals — divided by the acre rather than the standard city lot. That's how the community got its name. The area drew Black families with inexpensive land, low taxes, and the chance at an agrarian, self-built life at a time when housing inside Houston's city limits was often restricted by discriminatory covenants and lending practices. Residential development picked up in the late 1930s as developers including W.W. Mount and the Wright Land Company began building out the area; early residents often dug their own wells and built their own homes.

For decades, Acres Homes operated with its own churches, schools, businesses, and civic institutions as the largest unincorporated Black community in the Southern United States — entirely outside Houston's city limits. That changed when the City of Houston annexed roughly 725 acres of the area in 1967, followed by another 1,469 acres in 1974, bringing Acres Homes fully inside Houston's corporate boundary.

Sourced from: Texas State Historical Association, Handbook of Texas: Acres Homes, and the City of Houston's annexation history record. Timeline

From rural subdivision to Super Neighborhood

A rough shape of the community's history — every date below needs confirmation before publishing.

  • World War I era (c. 1917–18)

    Land platted in acre and half-acre lots

    Landowners subdivide rural land northwest of downtown Houston into large lots, marketed to Black families able to buy land, farm, and raise livestock at a time when housing options inside the city were often restricted by discriminatory covenants and lending practices.

  • Late 1930s–1960s

    A self-sufficient, unincorporated community

    Development accelerates as W.W. Mount and the Wright Land Company build out the area. Churches, schools, Black-owned businesses, and civic clubs grow up around the neighborhood while it remains the largest unincorporated Black community in the Southern U.S. — governed locally, informally, and by its own institutions.

  • 1967 & 1974

    Annexed into the City of Houston

    The City of Houston annexes roughly 725 acres of Acres Homes in 1967, then another 1,469 acres in 1974, bringing the community fully inside Houston's city limits. This is the pivotal legal fact for today's campaign — see The Process for why it matters.

  • Today

    Recognized as Super Neighborhood #12

    Houston's Super Neighborhood system gives Acres Homes an official advisory council, but final authority over budget, zoning, and services still sits with citywide government, not local residents.

Today

Acres Homes now

Still a close-knit, majority-Black community with deep multi-generational roots — and a growing Latino population — Acres Homes today is officially recognized by the City of Houston as Super Neighborhood #12.

⌂Housing Character

Large residential lots, single-family homes, and multi-generational households remain a defining feature, distinct from much of urban Houston.

⚖Civic Life

Long-standing churches, civic clubs, and a Super Neighborhood council give residents an organized voice — but only advisory power within Houston's government.

👥People

Generations of families who have owned land here since incorporation-era Houston didn't yet reach this far northwest.
